Honors Thesis

Tracking Bilingual Memory: How Crosslinguistic Activation Shapes the Neural Dynamics of Memory Formation

B.A. in Neuroscience and Linguistics, Northwestern University, 2026

Read the abstract

Psycholinguistic research has been central to understanding how bilingualism shapes the organization of the mind and brain. Previous work has shown that spoken word recognition involves the parallel activation of phonologically related lexical candidates. For bilinguals, this activation extends across both languages, thereby posing demands on cognitive processes such as attention and inhibitory control. However, whether these control demands extend to memory processes, such as encoding and retrieval, is still largely unexplored. To address this gap, we tested whether the co-activation of phonological labels that overlap within and across languages influences encoding during visual search and subsequent recognition memory.

Twenty-two Spanish-English bilinguals and 18 English monolinguals completed a visual search task manipulating within- and between-language phonological competition. On each trial, the display contained a target image and a competitor image that was either phonologically related to the target within the same language (e.g., battery /ˈbætəri/ and backpack /ˈbækˌpæk/) or phonologically related across languages (e.g., battery /ˈbætəri/ and balanza /baˈlanθa/, scale in English). Following the visual search task, participants completed a surprise memory test assessing recognition of previously-seen images. To examine how language co-activation influences subsequent item recognition, we analyzed eye movements, pupil dilation, and event-related potentials (ERPs). Eye movements provided indices of attentional allocation during visual search, pupil dilation indexed cognitive load and effort-related arousal, and ERPs captured the neural mechanisms underlying encoding and later recollection processes.

Eye movement results indicated that both groups were sensitive to within-language phonological competition in English relative to control conditions without phonological overlap. Bilinguals demonstrated attentional shifts toward between-language phonological competitors, indicating crosslinguistic co-activation. Additionally, bilinguals responded more slowly overall, suggesting greater processing demands. This behavioral pattern is consistent with the ERP findings, which showed enhanced N2 amplitudes (i.e., an index of increased conflict monitoring and cognitive control) for bilinguals during co-activation in the visual search task. Complementing this effect, pupillometry data from the recognition task revealed that bilinguals showed greater pupil dilation during the retrieval of within-language competitors relative to new distractors, reflecting increased cognitive effort when recognizing competitor items in English, their second language. Crucially, a larger LPP effect (i.e., an index of recollection-based memory retrieval) was found for bilinguals in the recognition of previous within-language competitor items, suggesting sustained attention and successful retrieval relative to newly presented distractor items.

Our findings demonstrate that crosslinguistic co-activation dynamically recruits attentional and control mechanisms that extend beyond language processing, influencing how information is encoded and later retrieved.
